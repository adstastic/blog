Quoting [Edmar Ferreira](https://every.to/source-code/has-ai-gotten-good-enough-to-predict-my-taste-i-had-to-know?ph_email=jan.klug%40gmail.com):

> universal discovery engine: an opposite of Google Search that would surface content you don&#39;t even know you want yet.

> our real preferences hide in the shadows—we read without liking, enjoy without sharing. The platforms that know us best guard their data like dragons. And taste itself shapeshifts through lived experience: A chef&#39;s palate emerges from years of burnt soufflés and perfect reductions, from connecting flavors to emotional memories—creating judgments no algorithm can fake.

> Our taste continually evolves, shifting with exposure to new influences, life experiences, and cultural trends. A music enthusiast who once dismissed electronica might develop an appreciation for it after attending a transformative live performance. A reader&#39;s literary preferences might expand dramatically during a period of personal upheaval.

> Every time I upvote an article or save an article to read later, I&#39;m making a judgment about whether that content is worth my attention.

> a &#34;taste rubric&#34;: a detailed description of what catches my attention and what doesn&#39;t.

> Instead of asking, &#34;Is this article interesting?&#34; I asked, &#34;Which of these two articles is more interesting?&#34; This mirrors how we actually make choices in real life, and the results were striking.

> When I saved an article about consciousness or a profile of an obscure philosopher, would I actually read it? The AI could tell I was interested in philosophy, but distinguishing between the essay that would captivate me for an hour versus one I&#39;d abandon after two paragraphs proved much harder.
