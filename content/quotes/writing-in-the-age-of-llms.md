Quoting [sh-reya.com](https://www.sh-reya.com/blog/ai-writing/?utm_source=tldrai):

> Empty “summary” sentences that pretend to conclude a thought

These often show up at the end of a paragraph and sound like:

•   “By following these steps, we achieve better performance.&#34;
•   &#34;By internalizing these principles, you can cut through the noise.”

Empty summary sentences feel conclusive, but say nothing. If we deleted them, nothing would change.

> Overuse of bullet points and outlines

LLMs default to structure, which often means bulleted lists. But not everything needs to be a list. Only use bullets when the items are truly parallel, unordered, and best understood as discrete pieces. Otherwise, a paragraph is better, since it lets us build relationships between the ideas, signal emphasis, and provide context.

> Flat sentence rhythm

Writing filled with sentences of the same length is monotonous, especially if they’re long or complex. Readers naturally find text with varied sentence lengths more engaging, as variation helps signal emphasis and guides attention.

> It sounds nice but says very little. The sentences are well-formed, but there’s no concrete insight, no framing, no momentum.

> AI writing often avoids specificity. It gestures at ideas without pinning anything down. E.g., *“Some experts say prompt engineering is becoming less important. The ability to simply prompt LLMs can have a major impact on productivity.”* What experts? What impact? On whom? The writing resists commitment, which makes it feel hollow.

> Overuse of demonstrative pronouns

This, that, these, those—they’re everywhere in AI writing, often with no clear noun attached. I make this mistake a lot myself, and my advisor circles it in my papers every time. E.g., *“This creates friction in production.”* What is *this*? If the noun isn’t in the same sentence or directly before it, the connection gets muddy.

> Fluency without understanding

Some writing sounds correct but doesn’t explain anything. This happens a lot when the writer—or the model—lacks awareness of what the audience actually knows.

> Repeating a key idea—especially when introducing a new concept—is often necessary. It helps the reader slow down and absorb. What matters is how the repetition works. If it reframes or reinforces, it’s doing its job; otherwise, repetition can be removed.

> Signposting phrases

Phrases like “essentially,” “in short,” “the point is…” are fine if they’re followed by something useful. I like to use them when the writing gets dense, as a signpost helps the reader reorient.

> Some patterns get labeled as “AI-sounding” or flagged during review as signs that a model wrote the draft. But not all of them are bad. Many are useful rhetorical tools, especially when used intentionally.

> Long sentences with control

AI gets a lot of flack for being too wordy, but short sentences aren’t always better. A longer sentence can be more efficient if it layers or stacks ideas cleanly.

**Example:** *“While earlier models treated the input as a flat string, our approach segments it hierarchically, allowing different strategies to be applied to each section type.”*

> Parallel structure

Sometimes readers see a repeated rhythm and assume it’s AI. But parallel structure can help organize related ideas and makes sentences easier to follow.

> Section headings that echo a structure

E.g., “Why X fails,” “What to do instead,” “How to know if it worked.” These are clear and predictable, which is what we desire. Predictability isn’t bad when the content under each heading is clear.

> Declarative openings

Starting a section with a bold claim or topic sentence can feel robotic if the writing doesn’t back it up. But when used to set expectations—and followed by evidence—it helps keep the reader grounded.

> Em dashes are great for inserting clarifying details, quick shifts, or sharp asides—without breaking the sentence. I love them. When used well, they add rhythm and emphasis. They help writing flow the way people actually talk.

> My writing loop is built around one goal: keep the momentum going. I don’t want to get stuck staring at a blank screen or endlessly tweaking sentences that don’t quite land.

> plan an outline (on paper or in my head), generate a draft, read what I wrote, critique it, and revise. The loop can run at different granularities—sometimes I work a sentence at a time; sometimes I write entire sections before editing.

> identify where the slowdown is happening and hand off just enough of the task to the LLM to regain momentum.

> Narrate the story to the model

When I start writing (especially for something like a paper intro), I begin by “talking through” the structure as if I’m explaining it to a colleague. I paste that rough narrative into the LLM and ask it to generate a detailed outline. I don’t move forward until that outline feels structurally solid.

> Write the paragraph myself, even if it’s rough

Once I have the outline, for every paragraph, I try to write the actual paragraph myself, even if it’s ugly. If I know what I want to say but can’t get the sentence out (unfortunately, this happens often), I’ll write a half-baked version and ask the LLM to help me finish it.

> When I re-read a sentence or paragraph that feels off, I don’t simply ask the model to “make it better.” I ask something specific; usually for the LLM to follow one of the following rhetorical patterns.

The first is to **put the subject and verb close together, at the beginning of the sentence**.

> **SWBST: Somebody Wanted But So Then**. It’s a basic storytelling structure—often taught in early writing education, but surprisingly effective in technical contexts. It captures motivation, conflict, and resolution in a compact way. For example:

&gt; We used GPT-4 for summarization. We wanted fluent answers, but it hallucinated facts. So we added a retrieval step. Then we re-ranked outputs based on citation accuracy.

Each sentence does one job. The pattern is simple, but it makes the logic of a decision easy to follow.

> It’s now cheap to generate medium-quality text—and even high-quality text, when the scope is narrow and well-defined. But figuring out what to say, how to frame it, and when and how to go deep is still the hard part. That’s what takes judgment, and that’s what LLMs can’t do for me (yet). So I use the LLM to just keep momentum, to push through stuck phrasing or refine a rough thought, but I stay in charge of the structure, the logic, and the point(s).

> Perhaps the most important mark of good writing—one that matters even more in the age of AI-generated text—is that the contribution is commensurate with the length. The reader walks away feeling their time was well spent, and this is the bar I strive to meet.

> This post details some of my thoughts on writing in a world where much of what we read is now machine-generated. First, I’ll lay out some common patterns of bad writing I see from AI tools. Then, I’ll defend some writing habits that people often dismiss as “AI-sounding” but are actually fine—even helpful—when used intentionally. Finally, I’ll share concrete rules and formulas I rely on in my own writing and in the prompts I use to guide AI.
